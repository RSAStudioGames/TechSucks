Server Hardware#

–CPU /  RAM / Storage (SSD/HDD) / Mobo / Networking / PSU / Chassis (Rackmount/Tower)–

Every server you build is gonna be comprised of a few select components and, while there are general-purpose systems that can handle most things you throw at them, they’ll never excel in any one area. Jack of all trades, master of none.

Processors#

At the heart of every system are your processors, handling the plethora of workloads you throw at them, ranging from HPC to AI/ML applications. There are a number of architectural differences in their core design that dictate where they excel in specific use-cases. In my experience, I haven’t found many major differences between CPUs in how they handle most workloads, but with GPUs it’s an entirely different story.

GPUs#

I’m going over the main points to look for in each of these general use-cases. I am generalizing a lot so you’ll have to do your own research, but the idea is that this can be a starting point for what you need with some recommendations. It’s a general overview so I’m not going too in-depth into the details.

Grouping into categories of different use-cases of GPUs and what to look for in those applications. Select hardware purpose-built for specific tasks. Like if you’re wanting to render stuff, then VRAM and memory bandwidth wont be your top focus as much as it would be with AI or HPC. For performance reasons, it isn’t recommended to run a normal x16 lanes GPU below x8 lanes. There’s a small drop at x8 from x16, but further than x8 will cause some fair performance drops with bandwidth-heavy workloads.

HPC / Medical & Scientific Visualization#

Generally HPC & Visualization tools need high amounts of VRAM to fit large amounts of data/datasets in the GPU to quickly simulate or process specific types of data. You’ll generally need GPUs with high FP64 performance because they need to be as accurate as possible to contribute relevant data. Memory bandwidth play a big part in this to quickly load and move data around in memory. You’ll want to look for cards with high VRAM and FP64 performance for this. P100 (Nvidia budget), V100 (Nvidia mid), MI100 (AMD mid), or A100 and up are the kinds of cards you’d want to look for. Depending on your use-case, you may need multiple cards with an NVLink/xGMI bridge.

  • Floating-Point Performance (Double Precision)
  • High VRAM capacity
  • High Memory Bandwidth
    • Including high-speed interconnects (NVLink & NVSwitch/Infinity Fabric Links)

Artificial Intelligence & Machine Learning#

If you’re running AI models in your server, VRAM and memory bandwidth are gonna be your main focuses with floating-point performance shortly after that. LLMs and video generation models will usually be quite VRAM hungry. Image generation models are more intensive on memory bandwidth and floating-point performance. So if you want to serve the best LLMs possible, you’re gonna want to look into getting something like P40s (budget Nvidia), MI50s (budget AMD), V100/T40/RTX6000 (Mid Nvidia), MI100 (Mid AMD), A100s These are some cards to look into that have good performance for their respective price ranges. A100s and up are going to be quite expensive and not worth the price for the average user unless you have money burning a hole in your pocket. Midtier cards like the V100/T40/RTX 6000 (Turing) series of cards have a good Price to Performance to VRAM ratio, but are still relatively costly. P40s and MI50s are significantly cheaper and have quite a bit of VRAM but their performance is not the best so it really depends on how you plan to serve LLMs. How many users, use-cases, quantized models are probably needed, how large of a model are you using, etc. Depending on your use-case, you may need multiple cards with an NVLink/xGMI bridge.

  • Tensor/Matrix/XMX cores for AI acceleration
  • Floating-Point Performance (FP16/BF16/FP8)
  • High VRAM Capacity
  • High Memory Bandwidth
    • Including high-speed interconnects (NVLink & NVSwitch/Infinity Fabric Links)

Professional Graphics / Real-Time Rendering & Animation#

For rendering & real-time graphics (like gaming), you want to look more for VRAM capacity with a good clock speed. Ray tracing cores are recommended as they may become required for modern gaming and good improvements in animation quality. VRAM needs to render a scene increase as they become larger and more complex. If you’re running a personal cloud gaming system… If you’re doing animation on your own… If you’re running a render farm for your own use… If you’re needing a good GPU for video processing or professional graphics…

  • VRAM Capacity
  • Ray-Tracing
  • Cores & Clock speed

AVOID DRAM-LESS SSDs AT ALL COSTS FOR IMPORTANT DATA! They have lower lifespans and are generally slower.

  • Recommended Brands/Models

I recommend looking up the data sheet for any SSD you plan on buying and look for the TBW value which tells you about how much data can be written to the drive before it is expected to fail. SSDs have a finite amount of data they can write before they no longer can write. You usually can still read the SSD at this point, but it will no longer be possible to write new data to it. The Samsung SSD 870 EVO 250GB model has a TBW of 150 terabytes. The 4TB version has a TBW of 2,400 TB. Used enterprise SSDs can also be a good choice, but always make sure to get their drive health before buying as you could potentially buy a dying drive. I don’t recommend buying used consumer SSDs. Also, when looking for used enterprise SSDs, make sure they are compatible with your system. SATA is SATA, but some of these SSDs use a bunch of different interfaces like E1.S or U.3. U.3/U.2 look similar to SAS or SATA connectors, but they will not work with SATA at all or just any SAS-card. I’ll be covering a lot more about this when I get some more time to finish this page. 2U and 4U servers are usually fairly quiet (excluding GPU servers) and have larger fans which means they can be quieter. 1U tends to be quite whiney and loud as they have to force air through the chassis with smaller fans which requires the fans to be louder and more powerful.

Server chassis fans (like in Dell and HP servers) cannot be replaced with quieter alternatives sadly as you need a fan with enough static pressure to keep the components cool. Something like a 40mm Noctua fan would not be able to push enough air into the system to keep it cool in the first place.

Dell R700 Series#

The R700 series is a 2U system that you can tune the fans to be fairly quiet (for a server). If you put one or two GPUs in it, then yes, it will get close to jet engine loud, but otherwise, I’d even be comfortable with the noise if it were my own workstation. The R730 has DDR4 and broadwell CPUs. Don’t go for the R720 as is not worth the money whatsoever, uses DDR3 memory, uses a lot more power, is louder, and is only marginally cheaper than the R730. The R710 is just as out-of-date and also uses PCIe Gen 2… TWO! That’s bloody old, borderline e-waste. If you can get an R740, get one as they use Skylake CPUs and are more efficient too.

Tower Systems#

For the tower systems, I would still consider 12th-gen systems because I would not recommend these for anything that requires high performance. Instead, I’d use these as a home cloud or media server rather than doing anything hefty. 12th-gen dell tower systems still use DDR3, but still work fairly well. I wouldn’t go for T100 systems as they don’t have as many features as something like a T300.
