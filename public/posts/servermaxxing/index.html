AI Server Builds#

Here are some server setups I’ll be covering that can run large models with budget prices. By budget I mean it won’t cost the same as a new Ferrari, which some SOTA systems can easily surpass. I’ll be going over specific hardware to look for in more detail over on the Server Building page. These aren’t branded products or off-the-shelf systems. I’ve curated these from community builds. I have personally built and tested multiple of these systems, while others are based on extensive research, real-world benchmarks, deep dives into component specs, and reports from users who’ve tried them out to give you an accurate picture of how they would work in a real-world situation.
You can find the all the benchmarks I’ve run myself and collected in the LLM Inference Benchmarks page.

Getting Started#

So you’ve got three main ways to run AI models. NVIDIA GPUs, AMD GPUs, or CPUs. Nvidia will give you the least headaches (usually), especially on windows. AMD will give you more headaches and somewhat lower performance than Nvidia GPUs, but you can get really good deals on them while having more VRAM than most Nvidia cards at the same, and sometimes higher price points. Finally, CPUs are simple to run LLMs on with llama.cpp-based inference tools (Text Gen Web UI, Ollama, etc), but they’re realllllllllllly slow compared to GPUs.

If you want to run an AI model on your own hardware, decommissioned data center GPUs or CPUs are the way to go if you don’t want to spend hella cash on some fancy new tech that is bound to be overpriced. That being said, GPUs and CPUs are already stupidly expensive. Crypto-mining, Nvidia and AMD being greedy (mainly Nvidia), the massive AI spike pushing demand toward datacenter GPUs over the tiny consumer market, and scalping due to low availability (again, thank Nvidia and AMD and their blatant marketing lies and low consumer card availability) have caused the GPU market to skyrocket in price for not only new, but used GPUs. Overall availability is at an all-time low while prices are insanely high.

There isn’t much support yet for NPUs/TPUs/APUs yet as both the software and hardware ecosystem in the consumer market isn’t that mature yet. I recently found an inference engine — Lemonade Server — which as of now, has support for the Ryzen AI 300 Series of NPUs. When it comes to TPUs, you can find hardware such as Google Coral for smaller-scale ML projects, but you won’t be running Stable Diffusion on it without a fair bit of tweaking.

Nvidia has CUDA, AMD has ROCm, and Intel has OneAPI.
CUDA is the most mature with the most well-developed features and support.
ROCm is lagging behind a bit but is quickly catching up to CUDA and Nvidia’s overall performance in the AI arena.
OneAPI… well, Intel has a long way to go, but in all fairness, they only joined the GPU market fairly recently in 2019 with Gaudi compared to Nvidia and AMD’s 25+ years headstart.

Tesla is Nvidia’s line of datacenter GPUs. These are fairly easy to find on the second-hand market, mainly on ebay. When you’re searching for these GPUs, you’re bound to find many with what seems like massive amounts of VRAM. This may seem great, but you need to be careful with your selection.

The Tesla K80 has 24GB of VRAM (it’s technically two GK210 GPUs on one card with 12GB VRAM each), and its Maxwell counterpart, the M40, also has 24GB. These two cards can work for AI, but I’d strongly recommend going for nothing less than a P40 if you’re looking to bulk up on VRAM for a low price.

The reason is because Maxwell (Ex: M40) and Kepler (Ex: K80) architectures don’t have native FP16 hardware acceleration, meaning running most LLMs, or the average AI tool in general, will be quite slow. Still faster than CPU processing, but slow nonetheless. In the subsequent Pascal architecture (also found in the GTX 10 series), Nvidia introduced limited support for half-precision (FP16), it is slow at FP16 processing, but the P40 will still outperform its predecessors enough to be worth the increased price, if you can get them for under $200.

That being said, for ~$50 more, you can find an AMD counterpart, the Instinct MI50, with 32GB of VRAM. Higher VRAM density in your budget servers can make a huge difference in power requirements, components needed to build your server, and overall cost.

There is a lot to be said about performance differences between ROCm and CUDA, but with budget cards such as these, it’s not going to make too much of a difference if you’re only using common GGUF/ExLlama/GPTQ quantizations for inference as ROCm just isn’t as well-supported as CUDA with most tools. You could also use ZLUDA to run CUDA-based tools on your AMD GPU to get around some limitations, but I can’t say for certain as I haven’t tried it personally yet.

Moving on from Nvidia’s Pascal generation, you start to see significant performance increases with Volta, primarily the V100 (although I’m sure the Titan V would give very similar results, albeit with 4GB less VRAM). I personally saw significantly faster performance over the P40 for both image and text generation inference.

  • 2x P40 - L3.3 70B @ 4BPW EXL2 - ~3 tokens/s (just a hair away from OOM) 48GB VRAM fits 4BPW Llama 3.3 Instruct with enough VRAM for 32K context. (About 47GB VRAM used)
  • 4x V100 16GB - L3.3 70B @ 4BPW EXL2 - ~12 tokens/s 64GB VRAM fits 4BPW Llama 3.3 Instruct with enough VRAM for 64K context. (About 60GB VRAM used)

One big reason for this big performance boost is because of the introduction of tensor cores; dedicated parts of the GPU die designed specifically to process tensor operations. Volta uses first generation tensor cores; the shape of the tensors are also different than the following generations, leading to lack of support with Flash Attention and other tools/features which require it.

Subsequent generations of tensor cores would increase GPU performance significantly for AI workloads. Since the Radeon Instinct cards directly competing against Nvidia’s Volta GPUs had no tensor core equivalent, they tended to have slower performance in AI use cases.

With the introduction of the Tesla P100, came the first generation of NVLink. This was created to address the bandwidth bottleneck of PCIe in HPC and AI/ML workloads. NVLink is a high-speed GPU-to-GPU link that provides significantly faster communication with lower latency compared to traditional PCIe connections. Each NVLink acts similarly to PCI lanes, so as later generations added more links, the overall bandwidth increased with it.

With PCIe, if one GPU needed to access the memory of another, the data would have to travel from the first GPU, through the PCIe bus to the CPU and RAM, then back through the PCIe bus to the second GPU. NVLink bypasses this bottleneck with a dedicated link allowing direct GPU-to-GPU communication. While PCIe 3.0 x16 offers only 16GB/s (and PCIe 5.0 x16 maxes out at 64GB/s), NVLink provides significantly higher bandwidth, starting at 160GB/s for first-gen and reaching up to 1.8TB/s aggregate bidirectional with 5th-gen. Real-world performance differs slightly and primarily depends on the GPU you’re using, the amount of GPUs, and a few other factors that I don’t feel like expanding on yet…

Above Image: NVLink 2-way bridge on 2x A100s

NVSwitch extends NVLink capabilities even further, effectively allowing for GPUs to be networked together across multiple nodes. NVLink allows for all-to-all GPU communication at full NVLink speeds, allowing for any GPU to communicate with any other GPU in the cluster. You will see this used a lot in large-scale AI training where memory bandwidth is vital for processing huge amounts of data at a time.

Hardware#

This setup will give you 4x V100s with 16GB of VRAM each on the SXM socket. You do not want PCIe for this because NVLink with 4x V100 PCIes are more expensive while also being slower than the SXM setup. You can also get the 32GB version of the V100 to further increase your VRAM capacity to 128GB. Additionally, you can also use InfiniBand NICs, using GPUDirect RDMA, to scale with multiple servers. The reason for building a system like this is to take advantage of tensor parallelism which will greatly increase your inference and training speeds on supported back-ends.

It’s tough to custom build an SXM NVLink GPU server because NVLink boards are not something you can just plug and play into any system. It’s possible to do so, but very difficult, and usually more expensive, to obtain the right parts, and you definitely won’t receive any official support. Sure, you can also go to ebay and get some custom-built NVLink PCBs for SXM sockets, but you will not get the same performance as just going for an existing platform. These systems use custom NVLink fabrics to link the GPUs together so your speeds will vary depending on what the board is using.

I don’t recommend the T180/T181 systems as they use OCP power (external 12V power) which means you either need to find an Open Compute Project server rack which supports this kind of power delivery system or you can some normal server power supplies, a breakout board, and (possibly multiple depending on wattage) server power supplies to get them working. However, the T180/T181 systems are relatively cheap as several sellers on ebay have a fairly large surplus of them. Dell C4140/C4130 systems tend to be a lot more rare, and for that reason, they are more expensive. Even for a barebones system you are probably gonna see listings around ~$3500+. I lucked out and got two fully kitted out C4140s with 4x V100s and 96GB of RAM for $1800 each.
The Dell C4140/C4130 systems both require C19 plugs and 20 AMP power. Do not try to push 20 AMPs of power through a 15 AMP cable. It’s both a fire hazard and an easy way to make an expensive paperweight. Their NVLink boards are also hard to find and expensive to buy if you need one.

If you want to go cheaper, you could use SXM2 P100s instead of V100s, but I wouldn’t recommend it as V100s are only about double the price (~$45 vs ~$100) than the P100 while being significantly faster. Be aware that you cannot NVLink different GPU architectures together. I cover what NVLink & NVSwitch do in a bit more detail in /machine-learning.

I highly recommend getting a cheap torque driver to make sure you aren’t over-tightening the heatsinks over the GPUs as too pressure can damage the boards, it has a tolerance of less than 5%. You could get away without using one, but I wouldn’t recommend taking the risk. I’ve heard many complains from datacenter techs about SXM GPUs being ridiculously brittle. A lot of the time, the manufacturer putting the system together has a custom jig to mount the GPUs to the NVLink board to avoid the pins getting accidentally bent as well. When I had to replace the NVLink board on one of my C4140s, it rattled me a bit hearing all the problems people had, but it wasn’t that bad. Just be slow, patient, and don’t rush the process. Taking out the GPUs from the old NVLink board, replacing the board in the chassis, then putting the GPUs and heatsinks back took about 45 minutes, but it was also my first time doing it. If you can avoid it, do so as it is a bit of a headache.

Powering Your System#

The Gigabyte T180-G20 and T181-G20 both require an (OCP) OpenComputeProject v1.0 rack and some power distribution busbars. The T180/T181 are a bit wider than traditional racks so you may need to modify your rack setup.
The other way of powering them is not ideal, but it’s another option that doesn’t require an expensive rack can’t use for most other systems easily.

HP PSU Breakout Board - Direct Link - This is what you’re looking for. Get some 14 AWG wire and a very small flathead screwdriver. You’ll then have to solder some higher gauge wires to each of the three busbar connectors. It takes a bit of work, but you’ll have to figure this out for yourself. Here’s a useful wire size calculator if you decide to take this path. Make sure you know the pinouts of whatever PSU or system you’re working with or you’ll be in for a nasty surprise.
OCP Pinout - Here’s the pinout for you to get started with.

Be aware, if you're on a 15 amp breaker, which you most likely are if you live in the USA, you need to watch how much power you're putting through the system. It is critical you don't try to pull more power than you're able to as it will trip your breaker and possibly cause hardware damage.

I have not done the breakout board method myself as I went with Dell C4140s and the expensive nightmare (likely will be easier for you) of getting 240v 20 amp L6-20R outlets installed. I went with L6-20R outlets over 5-20Rs as they have a unique design so normal appliances wouldn’t be accidentally plugged into them as they are 240 volt. I also thought they looked cooler. I then got these L6-20P to C19 adapter cables from StarTech, because they have a good reputation and quality products, to plug into my servers. The cable is rated for 250V and 20 amps so you won’t have to worry about any fire hazards if you’re using them correctly.
A quick rule of thumb is that if your PSU takes more than 1200W, you’re probably going to need a 240V 20 amp breaker to properly power it so you don’t risk tripping the breaker or potentially damaging your hardware.

Storage#

T180/T181 both have slots for 4x 2.5" drives and 2x expansion slots that could also hold PCIe SSDs. I would recommend using the 2.5" drive bays for storage and keeping the PCIe slots for Infiniband NICs if you plan on scaling with multiple nodes.
I highly recommend using SSDs for a system like this. The faster your read speed, the faster you can load models. If you plan on doing AI training, then go for SSDs. You only really need one, but I’d put in two and mirror them for redundancy in case something goes wrong with one. Not sure if the T180 or T181 have built-in raid functionality though as I have not personally tried them out.
As for the C4140/C4130, there aren’t any drive bays and instead need to use either a PCIe SSD or the Dell Boss card to hold up to 2x M.2 SSDs. I put them in raid 1 using the built-in raid functionality. Unless you get high capacity SSDs, which I didn’t, I’d recommend using either an external USB SSD you can plug into the server for additional storage. I used a combo of a USB SSD and a local file server to hold my AI models. I had a few headaches when loading from the file server over ethernet as it was slower than USB 3.0 transfer speeds, and would occasionally have some issues in loading causing me to have to reload the model taking nearly 10 - 15 minutes for a single 70B model to load. On the USB SSD, it was closer to about 1 - 2 minutes.
For either the Dell or Gigabyte systems, you’re going to have plenty of left-over PCI lanes should you go with an NVME SSD.

System Setup#

Alright, Linux all the way. I highly recommend using some flavor of Linux for this, I personally went with Ubuntu server 24.04 LTS as it’s simple to use and I’m familiar with Debian-based distributions. You do not want to use Windows as SXM datacenter GPU drivers are not supported on Windows, and even if they were, you would be wasting a fair bit of performance just by running Windows in the first place. You will get much better performance using Linux. Don’t use a GUI if you can avoid it, but if you’re doing this project, you probably already know that.
Use the Nvidia driver documentation to install the V100 drivers onto your chosen Linux distro or refer to my /Server GPU Driver Installation page.

Once installed, I recommend dropping the power limit to each GPU to 200W. You’ll lose close to ~10% of your max performance, but its worth saving 400W if you’re going to be running this 24/7. In my case, I only turn on the system when I need it so I left mine at 300W. Be aware that the V100s each idle at around 50W so you’ll be using around 250W at any given moment just by having the server on.
Check the topology of the GPUs using nvidia-smi topo -m to make sure NVLink is correctly working.

Software#

  • I use Tailscale to SSH into the system remotely and securely. Super simple to set up and supports most operating systems, including android and IOS devices.
  • Text Generation Web UI is fairly simple to get started with and will give you the least headaches, especially when compared to Aphrodite Engine. AE gets better performance, but in my experience with it, I’ve had constant problems, primarily LLVM errors and very long model load times.

Make sure you are set to UEFI, and NOT BIOS mode. You’ll get significantly faster boot times and better performance and hardware support overall.

You can enable and disable GPUs using the following commands. I’d recommend disabling ones you aren’t using to save power.
(## = GPU ID from lspci | grep NVIDIA or nvidia-smi):
Disable: sudo nvidia-smi -i 0000:##:00.0 -pm 0
Enable: sudo nvidia-smi drain -p 0000:##:00.0 -m 1 (use -m 0)

Additional Considerations#

  • The only real upgrade path from here is upgrading RAM, CPU, and upgrading from 16GB V100s to 32GB V100s. Besides the VRAM boost, changes in the CPU and RAM will be somewhat negligible.
  • These systems are major power hogs and costs will add up. Probably not worth the power bill.
  • Somewhat relevant link: https://smallformfactor.net/forum/threads/1000w-max-server-psu-mod-12v-external-brick.16282/

You have SXM2 versions of the A100 available with 40GB of VRAM. These are for automotive use so they are technically not supported, nor have I tried using one before. I have heard people having issues with these in their systems and SXM sockets being bricked. Personally, I would not recommend these, not only because of the risk of bricking your board, but the performance will not be much better than a V100 as these are cut-down versions of the A100, and it is also more than double the cost of a single node for only 8GB of VRAM more. Use at your own risk.

Scaling#

This isn’t for the faint of heart. You want to know what you’re doing before starting as it’s not a simple process. Be comfortable with general Linux usage, CLI navigation, installing GPU drivers, setting up an inference engine that supports distributed inference like vLLM or Aphrodite Engine, installing NIC and GPU Direct RDMA drivers, and ideally have some basic Docker experience.

If you’re looking to scale across multiple nodes, you’re gonna want to get a Mellanox NIC. Something like a 40GbE, or preferably, a 100GbE NIC. 40GbE NICs are hella cheap, about $15 - $20 used, whereas a 100GbE NIC will go for about $70+ used. You only have PCIe 3.0 so unfortunately, you can’t get faster NICs. 40GbE uses QSFP+, and 100GbE uses QSFP28 DAC (direct attach copper), but both also support fiber. Fiber optic be cheaper for longer cable lengths should you need that. Avoid 40GbE if possible as it will cause noticeable bottlenecks. If you want to hook up more than three nodes together, you’ll need an Infiniband switch or additional NICs. A 40GbE Infiniband switch will range from ~$50 to ~$200. For a 100GbE switch, a quick search shows them starting around ~$450 and rising quickly from there.

Refer to this gist as a starting point for installing GPU Direct RDMA drivers for your Mellanox NIC. Unfortunately, the links going to the Mellanox domain are dead in that gist. Here are some more reference links to get you started.
Official InfiniBand driver download link.
Official GPU Direct RDMA driver install documentation.

Your inference backend needs to support distributed inference, so do your research beforehand. vLLM and Aphrodite Engine are both viable options. I’d recommend Aphrodite Engine over vLLM as it’s nicer for non-professional use-cases. It’s highly recommended to use the Docker version of vLLM or Aphrodite Engine to ensure that your node installations are are exactly the same. If your install environment is not the same on each node, you will very likely run into some annoying problems.

You can read up on starting distributed inference with vLLM or Aphrodite Engine here. AE vLLM
Aphrodite Engine is a fork of vLLM with a consumer focus. Both platforms are very similar overall.

Few helpful points:

  • Dual-port infiniband cards do not support daisy-chaining. However, you can still connect up to three nodes (with dual-port NICs) together so they all have a direct connection to each other.
  • You want as similar as possible, preferably the same hardware, for each node. Your drivers and software must match exactly (same CUDA version, drivers, inference engine, python environment, version, etc)

Hardware#

I recommend getting a motherboard with 4 PCIe slots instead. MI100s are 2x slots wide so make sure the motherboard can accomodate 4. This way, you can use the AMD Infinity Fabric Link to increase their memory bandwidth and decrease total communication hops. This will speed up inference a ton. An Infinity Fabric Link is basically AMD’s version of an NVLink bridge. The Infinity Fabric Link is also quite hard to find and expensive because it was never sold as a standalone product, but instead was sold with the servers themselves.

The Infinity Fabric Link (the 4-GPU link) requires 4x MI100s to function. It will NOT work with three GPUs or fewer!

You’re gonna have to install ROCm on this thing since it uses AMD GPUs. Check out /Server GPU Driver Installation which includes the link to the ROCm documentation.

You need the ROCm SDK to run inference models. Meta package - rocm-hip-sdk

Flash Attention does work, but needs to be manually built.
Clone the repo, then cd into the downloaded folder.

Add gfx908 to allowed_archs = which allows Flash Attention to build on the Mi100 like this: 

def validate_and_update_archs(archs):
    # List of allowed architectures
    allowed_archs = ["native", "gfx90a", "gfx950", "gfx942", "gfx908"]
Then run python setup.py install
Expect to wait a few hours for it to install. I’d do it overnight or let it run in the background in its own multiplexer window.

As you can see from the average speed of 4 tokens/sec, the memory bandwidth is the primary limitation here. My motherboard is not able to add a 4th card (easily) so I can’t use the infinity fabric bridge (which requires 4 cards) to boost the bandwidth. I did not think to test MoE models at the time, but those would be ideal in situations where your inter-GPU communication is limited by PCIe. If it was a small model that could fit on a single card the speeds would be significantly higher, but take that same model and split it over multiple cards (without the GPU bridge) and your speeds would drop. The more context used, the further those speeds would drop. With empty context, I would get 6-8 tokens per second and it quickly drops from there.

Miqumaxx Box#

So you don’t want to deal with the volatile GPU market, running out of power on a PSU, needing multiple PSUs, getting 20 amp power installed, need a ton of loud jet-engine fans, raising your house’s average temperature, or running out of PCI lanes, then this is for you.

Hardware#

Epyc Turin would be better than Genoa as it is both newer and supports faster RAM (6000 MT/s and some up to 6400 MT/s), however, it is also significantly more expensive at the moment. Here’s a handy tool to calculate your memory bandwidth.

You want to get the fastest RAM that your CPU can support. The faster your RAM, the faster your inference.

Software#

Make sure you are set to UEFI, and NOT BIOS mode. You’ll get significantly faster boot times and better performance and hardware support overall.
You want to run Linux to squeeze out every last bit of performance out of this.
Dense models will be much slower than MoE models. MoE models will be your friend, and luckily, some of the best open source models available are MoE and can run on this build. (DeepSeek R1/V3, Kimi K2, GLM 4.7, MiniMax M2.1, etc)
Disable Transparent Hugepages for increased stability with AI inference. If you’re curious about how THP works in more details, check out this great post.
Make sure the xGMI is set to full speed (x4) in the bios. (xGMI is AMD’s Infinity Fabric)

Since you’ll have multiple CPUs, be sure to configure your NUMA nodes, this will give you a bit of a performance boost in most inference cases.

Hardware#

So this build is basically just three Tesla P40s in a dell tower server for ease of use.

Components:

  • Dell T7910 Barebones with heatsinks.
  • Your choice of LGA-2011 CPUs. It’s about $30 for a pair E5-2680 V4s
  • DDR4 2400 RAM. Preferably 8 sticks to fill all the memory channels.
  • Your choice of storage. Preferably an SSD.
  • 3x Tesla P40s (you will need a power adapter cable for EPS-12V to GPGPU power)
  • 3x 27" 2011 iMac GPU fans (BFB0812HD). One for each GPU, wire them to the 12v and ground wires on the GPGPU power adapter cable. You can attach the fans using foil duct tape (like the kind used for HVAC stuff)

On average during inference/training, you’d be hitting ~700 watts of power usage.

I would highly recommend MoE models for this build since they are not as heavy on memory bandwidth and would net you higher inference speeds than a dense model.

You can support ~70B MoE model just on the VRAM alone at around 4 bits per weight and get decent speeds. If you have enough RAM, you can push that to around ~200B before it gets both, way too slow for my preference (1-2 tokens per second for a ~150B MoE model), and the quantization has lowered precision too much for mostly coherent responses.

With dense models, you can hit around 8-10 tokens per second on llama 3 70B at Q5KM with 4K context. The more context you use, the slower it will get as memory bandwidth usage increases quickly. Since the P40 doesn’t support NVLink, much less even SLI, there is no way to improve this besides using NVLinked P100s which also wouldn’t be possible in this form factor and it would also cut your VRAM in at most, half. (P100s are 12GB/16GB VRAM cards)
